The System

CAGED — the whole neck, finally.

Every chord on guitar comes from five movable shapes: C, A, G, E, D. Learn them once and you can play any chord, anywhere on the neck. Here's how.

The idea

You already know how to play an open C chord. The shape of your fingers — that exact pattern of dots — can be moved up the neck (with a barre across the open strings) to play C-shape major chords in other keys. Same for A, G, E, and D shapes.

The five shapes connect like puzzle pieces around the neck. Play C with the open C shape (3rd fret root on A string). Then the A shape (root still on A, at fret 3 → that's another C). Then G shape, E shape, D shape — all giving you the chord C, just in different positions. Five places to play one chord.

The five shapes (shown as movable barre forms)
How to practice CAGED
  1. 01Pick one note. Say, C. Find C on the low E string (fret 8) and the A string (fret 3).
  2. 02Play C using the E shape at fret 8 (barre across, E-shape fingers). That's a C major chord with the root on the low E string.
  3. 03Move to the D shape at fret 10. Then C shape at fret 12. Then A shape at fret 15. Then G shape at fret 17. All the same chord, climbing the neck.
  4. 04Do it for G, then D, then A. Within a month, every key feels reachable from anywhere.
How Music Works

Theory, the useful parts.

Not the academic version — just enough to understand why chords sound good together, why songs are in a "key," and how to figure out songs by ear. Read top to bottom.

01

There are only 12 notes

All of Western music is built from twelve notes. After the twelfth, they repeat an octave higher. On guitar, one fret = one note = one "half step." Here's the full set:

A A♯/B♭ B C C♯/D♭ D D♯/E♭ E F F♯/G♭ G G♯/A♭

Two things to notice: the "black key" notes have two names (A♯ and B♭ are the same pitch — context decides which name). And there's no sharp between B–C or E–F. Those two pairs are only one fret apart. That quirk explains a lot of guitar.

02

Intervals — the distance between notes

An interval is just how many half-steps (frets) apart two notes are. These are the building blocks of every chord and scale. The important ones:

Half step (1 fret)minor 2nd
Whole step (2 frets)major 2nd
3 fretsminor 3rd
4 fretsmajor 3rd
5 fretsperfect 4th
7 fretsperfect 5th

The 3rd is the most important interval to know. A major 3rd (4 frets) sounds bright and happy. A minor 3rd (3 frets) sounds sad. That single fret of difference is what separates a major chord from a minor one.

03

The major scale — the ruler

Almost everything in theory is measured against the major scale. It's seven notes built with a fixed pattern of whole (W) and half (H) steps:

W – W – H – W – W – W – H

Start on C and follow that pattern and you get C D E F G A B — the white keys on a piano, no sharps or flats. That's why C major is the "home base" key. We number these notes 1 through 7:

1 C 2 D 3 E 4 F 5 G 6 A 7 B

Those numbers are the secret decoder ring for the next three sections.

04

How chords are built — stack 3rds

A basic chord (a "triad") is three notes: take a starting note, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next. In numbers, that's 1 – 3 – 5.

C major = C·E·G
Notes 1, 3, 5 of the C scale. The 3rd (E) is a major 3rd above C → happy.
C minor = C·E♭·G
Flatten that 3rd by one fret (E→E♭) and the chord turns sad. Everything else stays.

Add the 7th note and you get richer chords. This is the whole logic behind the Chord Finder:

Major 7 (dreamy)1 · 3 · 5 · 7
Dominant 7 (bluesy, wants to resolve)1 · 3 · 5 · ♭7
Minor 7 (smooth, mellow)1 · ♭3 · 5 · ♭7
Sus4 (suspended, unresolved)1 · 4 · 5
05

Keys & the Nashville number system

A "key" means a song mostly uses notes and chords from one scale. Build a chord on each note of the major scale and you get a fixed pattern of major and minor — the same in every key:

Number Iiiiii IVVvivii°
Quality majminmin majmajmindim
Key of G GAmBm CDEmF♯°
Key of C CDmEm FGAmB°

The I, IV, and V chords (the major ones) are the backbone of folk, blues, and rock. The vi is the relative minor. Most three- and four-chord songs are just some mix of I–IV–V–vi. Once you hear this, you'll start recognizing it everywhere.

06

The progressions behind everything

Because the number pattern is the same in every key, songwriters think in numbers. Here are the four you'll meet most — try them in the key of G (use the table above):

The "four-chord song" I – V – vi – IV
In G: G – D – Em – C. Hundreds of pop and rock hits. Wagon Wheel, countless others.
The 12-bar blues I – IV – V
In G: G – C – D in a set 12-bar pattern. All of blues and early rock 'n' roll.
The 50s / doo-wop I – vi – IV – V
In G: G – Em – C – D. Stand By Me and a thousand slow dances.
The folk turnaround I – IV – I – V
In G: G – C – G – D. Traditional folk and country songs everywhere.
07

The circle of fifths

Move 7 frets (a perfect 5th) and you land on the next key that's most closely related — it differs by just one sharp or flat. Arrange all 12 keys this way and they form a circle. Neighbors on the circle sound good together; that's why I, IV, and V (which sit next to each other here) are the friendliest chords.

Practical use: if a song's chords feel like they "belong together," they're probably neighbors on this circle. And if you want to find a song's key, look at which group of neighboring chords it uses.

08

Putting it to work — songs by ear

  1. 1.Find the key. Play along and find the chord that sounds like "home" — where the song wants to rest. That's the I.
  2. 2.Try the neighbors. The IV and V (and vi) are almost always next. Use the table — in the key of G, that's C, D, and Em.
  3. 3.Listen for the pattern. Most songs cycle a 4-chord loop. Once you catch it, the whole song unlocks.
  4. 4.Use a capo to match. If the singer's too high or low, slap a capo on and keep the same shapes. Theory still applies — you've just moved the whole thing up.

How to use this page

For soloing: start with the minor pentatonic, Box 1. Five notes, hard to play wrong. Memorize that shape, then add boxes outward.

For melodies & understanding: switch to the full Major or Natural Minor scale. These seven notes are what songs are actually built from — the pentatonic is just these with two notes removed.

The modes (Dorian, Mixolydian) are the same seven notes as a major scale started from a different point — they give different flavors. Dorian is the jazzy/folk minor sound; Mixolydian is the bluesy major sound.
